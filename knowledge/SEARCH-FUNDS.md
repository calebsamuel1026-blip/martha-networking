# Search funds: real deal work while you're still in school

## What a search fund is
A searcher (often one person, sometimes two partners, usually a few years out of business school or banking) raises
money from investors to find and buy one small, profitable company, then runs it as CEO. "Self-funded" searchers do
the same with their own money or an SBA loan. During the search they look at hundreds of businesses, and most of the
work is exactly what an analyst does: finding companies, reading financials, building quick models, writing up why a
deal is or isn't worth chasing.

## Why it matters for students
A mentor's advice that started this track: get real finance work on your résumé before big application cycles open.
Searchers are short on time and often take part-time, remote students to help with:
- **Deal sourcing:** building lists of companies that fit their thesis, finding owners' contact info, logging outreach.
- **Industry research:** one-page market maps, who the buyers and competitors are, what drives margins.
- **First-pass screens:** reading a teaser or CIM (the sale document) and summarizing the business and red flags.
- **Models:** a simple three-statement or debt-service model to see if a deal can work.
It's real transaction work you can describe in interviews ("I screened 40 home-services businesses for a search fund
and built the model on two of them").

## Where to find searchers
1. **Searchfunder.com** (free account): the Jobs tab lists searcher internship and analyst posts. Sign in yourself;
   Claude can read the posts with you in the browser and draft your note, but you send it.
2. **Investor portfolio pages:** search-fund investors list their current searchers with links to each fund's site
   (for example Relay Investments' portfolio list). Each fund site usually has a contact page with the searcher's email.
3. **University and accelerator pages:** business schools and search-fund accelerators publish current searchers.
4. **Podcasts and posts:** ETA (entrepreneurship through acquisition) podcasts list guests who are searching now.
5. **Acquisition announcements:** a searcher who just bought a company often needs analysis help as the new CEO.

## Rules (on top of RESEARCH.md)
- **The search must be live:** the fund's own site, a dated 2025-2026 post, or the investor's list shows it is active
  (or the acquisition closed recently). Investor lists mark old searches "past"; skip those unless their site is live.
- **One email per fund.** If there are two co-searchers, write to one and mention nothing about the other.
- **Published email only.** A fund's own generic address (hello@, info@) is acceptable when it's a one or two person
  fund and no personal address is printed.
- **Fit first:** searchers whose thesis matches something you've actually done (an industry, a job, a region) go first.

## How the email differs from a networking email
Same structure as WINNING-EMAILS.md, one change: the ask is direct, because the searcher can use help right now.
1. Opening line, then one precise thing of THEIRS: their thesis in their own words, why they chose to search, their
   prior job, or the company they just bought.
2. One plain sentence tying it to you: the one experience that fits their thesis best. No list, no bragging.
3. A short "why you" line.
4. **The ask, plainly:** "I'm a current sophomore at <School> studying <major>, and I'd love to help with your search
   part-time and remotely this fall and spring, whether that's sourcing and screening companies, industry research
   or first-pass models." Vary the wording across a batch, keep it this clear. Don't mention pay in the first email.
5. A short call ask, "Resume attached.", "Thanks for your time." 119-159 words. Subject: 2-3 words naming their thing
   + " | <Fund> Interest".

## When they say yes
- Agree on hours, tools (Excel or Google Sheets, a shared drive), and one first project with a date.
- Ask whether it's paid or for credit only when they bring up terms or before you start. Many searchers pay an hourly
  rate; some offer a stipend or a reference instead. Decide what you need before the call.
- Keep a log of every company you screen and every model you build. It becomes résumé bullets and interview stories.
